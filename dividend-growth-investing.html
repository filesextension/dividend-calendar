Dividend Growth Investing (DGI) Strategy & Yield on Cost Framework

The most common psychological trap among self-directed retail income investors is yield chasing: prioritizing equities with high current dividend yields (such as 8% or 10%) while disregarding underlying corporate balance sheet health and distribution growth potential.

Professional dividend allocators utilize a different paradigm: Dividend Growth Investing (DGI). Rather than seeking maximum cash yield today, DGI focuses on high-quality companies with sustainable competitive advantages (economic moats) that consistently grow their dividend per share year after year. Over a multi-decade investing horizon, dividend growth systematically generates superior total returns and higher effective yield on initial capital.

1. The Yield on Cost (YoC) Phenomenon Explained

When you evaluate a stock on financial screening portals, you see its Current Indicated Market Yield. However, long-term investors track their real cash flow return using Yield on Cost (YoC):

Yield on Cost (YoC) = (Current Annual Dividend Per Share / Original Share Purchase Price) × 100

While the current market yield fluctuates daily based on changing stock prices, your cost basis remains fixed. As the corporate board expands the annual dividend distribution, your personal cash return against your initial capital investment rises steadily.

Real-World Mathematical Example: A 10-Year Compounding Case Study

Imagine two investors allocating $10,000 in Year 0:

  • Investor A (High Static Yield): Buys a mature utility yielding 8.0% with 0% annual dividend growth.
  • Investor B (Dividend Growth Champion): Buys a leading consumer staple or tech leader yielding 3.0% with an average 10% annual dividend growth rate.

2. Quantitative Screening: The Chowder Rule

To identify companies that strike the optimal mathematical balance between current yield and future dividend growth, legendary income analyst Lowell Miller formulated The Chowder Rule:

Chowder Number = Current Dividend Yield (%) + 5-Year Compound Annual Dividend Growth Rate (%)

Standard Chowder Thresholds:

  • Broad Market Equities (Yield < 3.0%): Requires a Chowder Number of at least 12.0%. For example, a tech champion yielding 2.0% must demonstrate a 5-year dividend growth rate of 10.0% or higher.
  • High-Yield Equities / Utilities / REITs (Yield > 3.0%): Requires a Chowder Number of at least 8.0%. For example, a premier REIT yielding 4.5% must demonstrate a 5-year dividend growth rate of at least 3.5%.

Frequently Asked Questions About Dividend Growth Investing

What is the core philosophy of Dividend Growth Investing (DGI)?

The core philosophy of DGI is investing in fundamentally robust, cash-generative businesses that consistently expand their cash dividend payouts annually, irrespective of broader stock market price volatility. The objective is generating an exponentially growing, inflation-protected stream of passive income.

How is Yield on Cost (YoC) calculated?

Yield on Cost is calculated as: (Current Annual Dividend Per Share / Original Purchase Price Per Share) multiplied by 100. Unlike current market yield, YoC measures the cash return generated relative to your original invested capital basis.

What is the Chowder Rule and how do you calculate it?

The Chowder Rule is a screening benchmark created by investor Lowell Miller. The formula is: Current Dividend Yield (%) + 5-Year Compound Annual Dividend Growth Rate (%). For standard broad-market equities, a Chowder Number of 12% or higher indicates an attractive candidate for long-term total return and compounding.

Why is a 2.5% growing dividend often superior to an 8% static dividend?

A static 8% dividend suffers real purchasing power destruction from annual inflation and typically offers minimal capital appreciation. Conversely, a 2.5% yield growing payouts at 10% annually doubles its cash distribution every 7.2 years, driving both substantial capital appreciation and an expanding Yield on Cost.

How does Dividend Growth Investing protect against inflation?

High-quality dividend growers possess structural pricing power, enabling them to raise product prices as general consumer inflation accelerates. This expands operational gross profits, allowing executive management to hike dividends faster than the consumer price index (CPI).
