Understanding Dividend Taxation in 2026

The U.S. tax code treats dividends very differently depending on how the paying company is structured and how long you have held the underlying stock:

Dividend Category Federal Tax Rates Holding Period Requirement Common Examples
Qualified Dividends 0%, 15%, or 20% Held > 60 days in a 121-day window around ex-date Apple (AAPL), Coca-Cola (KO), SCHD, VYM
Ordinary Dividends 10% up to 37% None (always ordinary income) Realty Income (O), Main Street Capital (MAIN), JEPI
International (W-8BEN) 15% treaty rate (or 30% default) Subject to country bilateral tax treaties Non-U.S. investors investing in U.S. equities

1. The 60-Day Holding Period Rule for Qualified Dividends

To benefit from favorable capital gains rates, common stock dividends must satisfy the IRS holding period test: you must hold the stock unhedged for more than 60 days during a 121-day period centered on the ex-dividend date. Short-term dividend capture traders who buy shares one day before ex-date and sell immediately afterward fail this test, subjecting their distributions to ordinary marginal income taxes.

2. Why REITs and BDCs Are Taxed as Ordinary Income

Real Estate Investment Trusts and Business Development Companies are pass-through entities exempt from corporate-level federal tax as long as they distribute at least 90% of their taxable earnings to shareholders. Because these profits avoid corporate income taxation, the IRS taxes distributions to shareholders at ordinary individual rates. Read our deep-dive on REITs & BDCs.

3. The Section 199A QBI Deduction for REITs

To reduce tax friction on domestic commercial real estate investments, individual U.S. taxpayers holding REITs in taxable brokerage accounts can take advantage of the Section 199A Qualified Business Income (QBI) deduction. This rule allows investors to deduct up to 20% of qualified ordinary REIT dividends on their tax return, effectively lowering the maximum tax rate on REIT distributions.

4. Asset Location: Optimizing Tax-Advantaged Accounts

Savvy dividend investors optimize net returns by aligning asset types with account structures:

  • Taxable Brokerage Accounts: Ideal for qualified dividend growth equities and index ETFs (like SCHD) that benefit from preferential 0% or 15% capital gains rates.
  • Roth IRAs / 401(k)s: The ideal shelter for high-yielding ordinary income assets like REITs, BDCs, and covered-call ETFs (JEPI/JEPQ), allowing distributions to compound completely tax-free.

Frequently Asked Questions About Dividend Taxes

How are qualified dividends taxed compared to ordinary dividends?

Qualified dividends receive preferential long-term capital gains tax rates (0%, 15%, or 20%), provided the investor satisfies the IRS 60-day holding period test. Ordinary non-qualified dividends are taxed at your standard federal marginal income tax rate (ranging from 10% up to 37%).

Why are REIT and BDC dividends taxed at ordinary rates?

Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs) and Business Development Companies (BDCs) pay zero corporate income tax under U.S. law by distributing at least 90% of their taxable income to shareholders. Because these profits were never taxed at the corporate level, the IRS taxes the distributions as ordinary income to shareholders.

What is the Section 199A deduction for REIT dividends?

Under the Section 199A Qualified Business Income (QBI) provision, eligible individual taxpayers holding REITs in taxable accounts can deduct up to 20% of their ordinary REIT dividends, effectively reducing their top marginal tax rate on those distributions.

How does U.S. withholding work for international investors with Form W-8BEN?

Non-U.S. investors are subject to a standard 30% statutory withholding tax on U.S. dividend income. If your country of residence maintains a bilateral tax treaty with the United States, submitting Form W-8BEN to your brokerage typically reduces this rate to 15%.

How does tax drag affect long-term dividend reinvestment (DRIP)?

Dividends held in taxable accounts are taxed in the year they are distributed, even when automatically reinvested via DRIP. Paying taxes out of pocket or reducing reinvestment amounts creates tax drag, which is why locating high-yield ordinary income assets in tax-advantaged accounts (like a Roth IRA or 401k) significantly improves compounding velocity.
