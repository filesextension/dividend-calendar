Yield on Cost (YoC) Calculator: Projecting Compounded Dividend Growth

While standard financial websites display the current dividend yield of a stock based on today's fluctuating market price, long-term buy-and-hold income investors focus on a more meaningful metric: Yield on Cost (YoC).

Yield on Cost measures the cash income you receive relative to the original capital basis you deployed. When you invest in companies that consistently increase their dividend payouts each year, your effective Yield on Cost expands exponentially, converting modest starting yields into double-digit passive cash flow machines.

The Mathematics of Yield on Cost

Yield on Cost isolates your personal economic return from broader market equity price fluctuations. The mathematical formulation is defined as:

Yield on Cost (YoC) = (Current Annual Dividend Per Share / Original Purchase Price Per Share) × 100

To project what your Yield on Cost will be in n years assuming a constant Dividend Growth Rate (DGR), the compound growth equation is:

Future YoC = Initial Yield (%) × (1 + DGR)Years

Why High Starting Yield Often Loses to Dividend Growth

Consider the classic dilemma faced by income investors: choosing between a 7.0% high static yield (common in debt-heavy utilities or high-risk business development companies) versus a 3.0% yield growing at 10% annually (typical of quality dividend growth leaders):

  • By Year 1: The 7% stock generates $700 on a $10,000 investment compared to just $300 for the dividend grower.
  • By Year 9: The dividend grower has doubled its distribution, paying out over $707 annually (a 7.07% YoC).
  • By Year 15: The dividend grower generates $1,253 annually (a 12.5% YoC), while the static utility is still paying $700.
  • By Year 20: The dividend grower generates $2,018 annually (over a 20.1% Yield on Cost), more than doubling the cash flow of the high-yield stock every single year thereafter.

This dynamic illustrates the core principle of Dividend Growth Investing (DGI): dividend growth champions defend your capital against inflation and create higher long-term income than chasing stagnant high-yield assets.

Frequently Asked Questions About Yield on Cost

What is Yield on Cost (YoC)?

Yield on Cost is a financial metric that measures an investor's annual dividend cash flow relative to their original investment purchase price, rather than the current fluctuating market price of the stock. The formula is: (Current Annual Dividend Per Share / Original Purchase Price Per Share) multiplied by 100.

How does dividend growth increase your future Yield on Cost?

Because your original cost basis remains constant, any corporate decision to increase the cash dividend per share directly increases your personal Yield on Cost. For instance, if you purchase an equity at $100 with a $3.00 dividend (3% yield), and the company raises its dividend to $6.00 over seven years, your Yield on Cost doubles to 6.0% on original capital.

What is the difference between current dividend yield and Yield on Cost?

Current dividend yield is a market-wide metric based on the prevailing stock quote today (Annual Dividend / Current Stock Price). Yield on Cost is an investor-specific metric reflecting return on actual capital deployed (Annual Dividend / Original Purchase Price). Current yield changes every second with market trading, while your cost basis never changes.

Can Yield on Cost exceed 100%?

Yes. Over multi-decade holding periods, legendary dividend growth companies can generate a Yield on Cost exceeding 100%. For example, long-term shareholders who bought Coca-Cola (KO) or Altria (MO) decades ago now receive more than 100% of their initial purchase price in cash dividends every single year.

What is a realistic Dividend Growth Rate (DGR) to use in projections?

For mature, defensive blue-chip companies (like Procter & Gamble or Johnson & Johnson), an annualized dividend growth rate of 5% to 7% is realistic. For faster-growing dividend champions (like Visa, Broadcom, or Microsoft), rates of 10% to 15% are common, though growth typically slows as enterprises expand.
